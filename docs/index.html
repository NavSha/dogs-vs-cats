When a computer “sees” a photo of a dog, it doesn’t see a dog. It sees a grid of numbers. Everything else — the ears, the fur, the verdict — is built up from those numbers, one layer at a time. This page lets you watch that happen, on a photo of your choosing, using a model that is genuinely running in your browser right now.

1 A photo is just numbers

Before a machine can tell a dog from a cat, the photo has to become something it can do arithmetic on. So the very first thing it does is shrink your image down to a tiny fixed size — this model uses 150×150 — and turn every pixel into three numbers: how much red, green, and blue it contains, each from 0 to 255.

That’s it. The image below is your photo at the exact size the model receives it. Move your mouse over it (or tap, on a phone) to read the three numbers hiding inside any single pixel.

2 The machine looks for patterns

A grid of numbers isn’t much to go on. So the machine passes your photo through a stack of layers, and each one hunts for a slightly more complicated pattern than the last. The earliest layers find almost nothing interesting — just edges. Later ones combine those edges into textures, then shapes.

4 How did it learn?

This machine wasn’t programmed with rules about whiskers and snouts. It was trained — shown thousands of labelled photos, over and over, nudging itself a little closer to right each time. Here is its actual report card.

Read the chart as a story. In the first stretch, the machine learns fast — then flattens out around 96%, no matter how many more times it studies. That flat line is a wall. To get past it, the training switches strategy at the dashed line: it gently unfreezes the deeper vision machinery it had borrowed and lets it adjust, just a little. That’s fine-tuning, and it buys the last hard percent — up to 97%.

Why the “real test” line is the one that matters
The machine is graded two ways. One score is on the photos it studied — easy, it could just memorise those. The honest score is on photos it has never seen. That blue line is the only one that tells you whether it actually learned to recognise dogs and cats, versus just memorising 20,000 specific pictures.
Wait — why is the “studied” score lower?
Odd, right? During practice the machine trains with a handicap: it’s shown crops, rotations and flips of each photo, with part of its own brain switched off at random. That makes practice harder than the real test on purpose, so it can’t lean on memorising. Take the handicap off and it does even better.
It knew when to stop
The second phase was allowed to run for 20 epochs, but it stopped itself after 8 — because the honest score had quit improving. Training longer wouldn’t have made it smarter; it would just have started memorising. Knowing when more effort stops paying off is a skill, for machines and teams alike.

5 Break it

Here is the most important thing to understand about this machine, and about most AI: it will answer every question with total confidence, even when the question makes no sense. It has only ever learned two words. So show it something that is neither a dog nor a cat, and watch what happens.

The machine has exactly two words in its vocabulary: “dog” and “cat.” It physically cannot answer anything else.

This is the lesson worth carrying out of this page. The machine isn’t lying and it isn’t broken. It is doing exactly what it was built to do: sort the world into the only two boxes it has ever known. Show it a fox and it will find the box that fits best, and report its confidence in that choice — not in whether the choice made any sense.

What this teaches you about all AI
Confidence is not correctness. A model can be 99% sure and completely wrong, because it’s measuring “how well does this match what I’ve seen” — not “is this true.”

A model only knows the world it was trained on. This one saw dogs and cats. The big ones saw much more, but the principle is identical: outside their training, they don’t know what they don’t know.

“I don’t know” has to be designed in. Nothing here ever says “that’s not a pet,” because no one built that option. If you ship AI, the most important question isn’t “how accurate is it?’ — it’s “what does it do when it’s handed something it was never trained for?”

You just watched a real neural network turn a photo into numbers, hunt for patterns, decide where to look, learn from twenty thousand examples, and then fail with total confidence. That whole arc — from pixels to a confident mistake — is the same arc running inside the much larger AI systems you use every day. Now you’ve seen it happen, end to end, with your own photo.
